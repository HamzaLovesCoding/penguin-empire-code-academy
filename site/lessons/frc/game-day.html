Unit 12

At the Competition

1 Why this matters

Your job is not to coach the driver

This surprises people, so it is worth saying plainly and early. During a match, the programmer's job is three things.

  • Not telling the driver what to do. That is the drive coach's role, and two voices in a driver's ear is worse than one.
  • Watching the console for errors. A line reading CAN ID 16 timed out means a wrong ID or, more often, a wiring break. And you now know that ID 16 is the back-left steering module.
  • Answering the driver's questions. "Did the arm actually go up?" is a question only you can answer, because you are the one looking at the numbers.

It is a quieter job than people expect, and a more useful one. The team's most valuable ninety seconds are the ones between matches, and what you noticed during the match is what makes those count.

2 What is different at a competition

Three things, and all three of them are yours.

  • The radio must be imaged at the field's setup station before you play, with a different configuration from the one you use at home. And you re-image it at home afterwards, or nothing works in the shop on Monday.
  • You go to the field with the drive team, carrying the driver-station laptop.
  • You plug in three things: Ethernet to the field, the controller over USB, and power.
Verify for current season

The radio imaging procedure is the most likely item on this page to have changed. Radio hardware and its setup flow have both moved between seasons, and so has how a driver station connects to the field.

Check this season's documentation and ask an experienced team member before your first event. The rest of this page, meaning the checklist discipline and the triage reasoning, does not change.

3 Only one laptop can do this

From Unit 2: the Driver Station is Windows only. The team's Macs cannot enable the robot. Three consequences that bite at eight in the morning.

  • Everyone's code has to reach that laptop before the first match.
  • That laptop has to be charged, and its charger has to be in the bag.
  • In a pinch you can write code on it.
Whatever you do not have, the show goes on

One of this team's captains had his own laptop die mid-season and wrote code on the driver-station laptop for the rest of the year. The robot did not care. Neither did the code.

If you are worried that you do not have the right machine, or the right setup, or enough experience: that is worth remembering. The constraints are almost always smaller than they feel.

4 The pre-match checklist

This is the central artifact of the lesson. It expands the bring-up checklist you wrote in Unit 2 with a competition's constraints.

In the pit

  1. Correct code deployed to the robot. Confirm the version, do not assume.
  2. Charged battery, secured.
  3. Limelight re-tuned for this venue's lighting.
  4. Autonomous routine selected in the chooser.
  5. Mechanisms move by hand. Nothing jammed from the last match.

At the field

  1. Robot placed on the marked start position, correct heading.
  2. Plug in Ethernet, controller and power.
  3. Driver Station open. Controller assigned to port 0.
  4. Communication green.
  5. Robot Code green.
  6. Joystick green.
  7. Console clear of errors before the match starts.

After the match

  1. Write down what you saw while you still remember it.
  2. Fix what you can in the pit. Note what you cannot.
During the match: watch the console, not the robot

Everyone watches the robot. Every single person in the stands is watching the robot, and your teammates are watching it too.

Nobody else is watching the console, and it is the only place a fault announces itself. Note anything that appears, along with roughly when it appeared.

6 Isolation, faster

The four-step procedure from Unit 2 still applies, and it matters more here than it did in the shop.

1. Turn the mechanism by hand with the robot off. -> is it mechanically free? 2. Apply a test voltage from the vendor client. -> does the motor turn at all? 3. Does the client see the device on the bus? -> is it addressed correctly? 4. THEN read your code. -> and only then.

Between matches you have minutes rather than hours. Cheap checks that eliminate whole categories of cause are worth more under time pressure, not less. Reading code first is the slowest possible approach, and it is what everyone does when they are panicking.

7 What to change between matches

DoDo not
Fix a clear, understood fault: a broken wire, a wrong constant, a missing timeoutDeploy a change you have not tested because you think it will help
Switch to the simpler autonomous routine if the complex one failed and you do not know whyRe-tune PID at a competition unless something mechanical actually changed
At a competition, prefer a known-working robot over a better one

An untested deploy between matches is how a working robot becomes a broken one, and you will not find out which you have until the match has already started.

The tuning story from Unit 6, with the bent parts and the mechanism that came off, happens fastest under pressure. Six minutes and an audience is the worst possible environment for changing a gain.

9 Common mistakes

Watch out for these
  • Coaching the driver. The drive coach coaches. You watch the console and answer questions, which is the job nobody else is doing.
  • Thinking there is nothing to watch when the robot is working. The console is where a fault announces itself before it becomes a failure. A timeout in match three is why you check a connector before match four.
  • Believing you can fix anything between matches. You have minutes. Prefer switching to a known-working configuration over deploying an untested fix.
  • Imaging the radio only once. Imaged at the field for the competition, re-imaged at home afterwards. Both directions.
  • Assuming any laptop can run the match. One Windows laptop, charged, with its charger in the bag.
  • Assuming vision will work because it worked in the shop. Different lighting. Re-tune at the venue.

Reveal the solution

1. Pit

  • Correct code deployed. Check the version, do not assume the last person deployed.
  • Battery charged and secured.
  • Arm moves freely by hand through its full range. Intake roller turns freely.
  • Limelight re-tuned for this venue's lighting.
  • The scoring tag IDs in VisionConstants match this field's tags.
  • Autonomous routine chosen, and if in doubt set to the simple leave-the-line one.
  • Charger and spare battery in the cart.

2. Field

  • Robot on the marked start position, correct heading. The autonomous start pose depends on it.
  • Plug in Ethernet, controller, power.
  • Driver Station open, controller dragged into port 0.
  • Communication green, waiting one to two minutes if not.
  • Robot Code green. Joystick green.
  • Console clear before the match starts.
  • The chooser shows the routine we actually intend.

3. During the match. Watch the console, not the robot. Write down any line that appears, with roughly when. Answer the driver if asked. Otherwise stay quiet.

4. Post-match. Write it down before you talk to anyone, because you will forget within ninety seconds. What appeared in the console, did autonomous do what it should, did anything behave oddly. Graph the arm and intake current if a mechanism cut out. Clear sticky faults only after you have found the cause.

6. Three things I will not do

  1. Deploy an untested change between matches. A robot that works imperfectly beats one that does not work at all, and I will not know which I have until the match starts.
  2. Re-tune PID at a competition unless something mechanical actually changed. Bad gains bend parts, and I would be doing it in six minutes under pressure.
  3. Coach the driver. That is the drive coach's job. Mine is the console, and if I am talking I am not reading it.

10 Closing the course

Twelve units ago you had never created a motor object.

PenguinBot now runs an intake, reads its own arm position, holds a setpoint under closed-loop control, sequences behaviours with real end conditions, responds to a driver, drives field-centrically, aims itself at an AprilTag, and runs a full scoring routine with nobody touching the controls.

Every one of those is a thing your team's robot needs somebody to do.
