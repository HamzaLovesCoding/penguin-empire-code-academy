1 Why this matters

Every season, this team breaks something with bad PID

That is not a warning we invented for a lesson. It is what has happened, every year. Bent parts. On one occasion an entire mechanism came off the robot.

Here is the mechanism of the damage, because it explains how a number in a text file destroys hardware. Set the gain too high and the arm reaches the setpoint still travelling fast. It overshoots. Now the error is large in the other direction, so the controller commands full power backwards. It overshoots again. The mechanism is no longer moving to a position. It is being driven at high power in alternating directions, fifty times a second, and every one of those reversals is a shock load through the gearbox and the mounting bolts.

You will produce exactly this behaviour in the sandbox below, and the harness will count the reversals for you. Nothing breaks on a screen. On a real arm something does.

The other half of the advice, and it is the part people skip: be patient, and change one variable at a time.

2 The recipe

This is the team's method. It is short, and you have to do the steps in order.

  1. Set kI and kD to zero. You are tuning one number, not three.
  2. Start kP very small and raise it slowly. Doubling each time is a reasonable habit.
  3. Stop when it reaches the setpoint quickly and cleanly. For most mechanisms you are finished here, and you never touch the other two gains.
  4. If it oscillates or will not settle, add kD.
  5. If it stops slightly short and stays there, add a small kI. Small means far smaller than kP, often by a factor of a hundred or more.
  6. Change only one variable at a time.
  7. Be patient.
Why step 6 is not optional

Change two gains, then see an improvement. Improved because of which one? You cannot say. To find out you now have to test both separately, which is the work you were trying to skip. You spent time and learned nothing.

With three gains and no discipline you are searching a three dimensional space by guessing. With the recipe you are searching one dimension at a time, and you can always answer the question "what did that change do."

3 Define done before you start

Write down what success is, with numbers in it, before you touch a gain. Without that sentence you will tune forever, because "better" has no definition.

The sentence for PenguinBot's arm

"The arm reaches the scoring setpoint within tolerance and stays there, in under a second and a half, without exceeding 0.8 output and without reversing direction."

Three of those numbers deserve names, because you will use them for the rest of your FRC career.

tolerance
How close counts as arrived. Without it, "at the setpoint" is never true.
settling time
How long until the mechanism is inside tolerance and stays there.
overshoot
How far past the setpoint it went before coming back.

Note the word stays in the definition of settling time. A mechanism that touches the target and then leaves has not settled. The harness below enforces that: if the position leaves the tolerance band, the settle counter resets.

Look at run 5 again, because it is a trap

By the two numbers most people check, run 5 is the best run on this page. It settled fastest, at 1140 ms. Its overshoot was lower than run 4's. The position column ends flat on 50.00. If you judged it on a position plot you would ship it.

Now read the third number. 23 direction changes. The motor was commanded forwards, then backwards, then forwards again, twenty three times, most of them after the arm had already arrived. The mechanism is not holding a position. It is vibrating against the setpoint, and every reversal is a shock load through the gearbox and the mounting hardware.

This is what takes mechanisms off robots, and it is why settling time alone is not a tuning target. A gain that arrives fastest and hammers itself is not tuned.

Raise it further and it gets worse in exactly the way you would expect. At kP = 25 the harness counts 42 direction changes over the same 150 ticks.

Where the simulator is kinder than reality

In PenguinSim run 5 does eventually settle, because the model has smooth damping and nothing else. A real mechanism has gearbox backlash, a flexing frame and a battery that sags under repeated full-power reversals. Those add energy back into the oscillation instead of removing it, which is why on real hardware this case often does not settle at all. Treat the sim's "settled at 1140 ms" as optimistic, not as permission.

6 What the clamp hides

The harness clamps output to 0.8 before it reaches the motor. That clamp is doing real work, and it is worth knowing exactly what work.

Early in a large move the controller is saturated. With kP = 0.2 and an error of 50, the controller asks for 10.0 and gets 0.8. It stays pinned at 0.8 until the error drops below 4. During that stretch the closed loop is temporarily indistinguishable from open loop, which is why the first rows of every run above look like a straight line, and why runs 3, 4 and 5 have identical positions at tick 15. This is normal.

The clamp limits force. It does not fix a gain. Prove it. Take run 5 and delete the clamp line entirely.

the experimentJava
double kP = 5.0;

double error  = TARGET - arm.getEncoder().getPosition();
double output = kP * error;
// output = Math.max(-MAX_OUT, Math.min(MAX_OUT, output));   // deleted on purpose
arm.set(output);
output
[sim] !! set(250.00) is outside -1.0..1.0 on CAN 3 - clamped to 1.0.
[sim] !! 100% output on CAN 3. 2551 fried a motor at ~90% - afterwards it
       ran about 5 seconds and quit. Stay under 80%, or add a second motor.
...
peak 55.30   overshoot 10.6%   direction changes 23
settled at tick 60  (1200 ms)

Compare that with the clamped run 5, which peaked at 53.79 with 23 direction changes.

  • The overshoot got worse, 10.6 percent against 7.6 percent.
  • The motor was commanded to 250 percent output on the first cycle, and the fried-motor warning from Unit 4 fired.
  • The direction changes did not move at all. Twenty three, either way.

That last point is the lesson. The clamp reduced how hard the mechanism hit itself. It did not reduce how often. Clamping is a damage limiter, not a tuning tool, and a bad gain is still a bad gain underneath it.

7 The sim gives you the method, the robot gives you the numbers

Gains describe a specific mechanism. They depend on its weight, its gearing, its friction and how much of it is fighting gravity. Change any of those and the numbers change with them.

  • An arm tuned empty is not tuned while holding a game piece.
  • An arm tuned on blocks in the shop is not tuned once the robot is on the floor with a full battery.
  • A new gearbox means a new tune. So does adding weight to the end of the arm.
  • The arm's gains are not the drivetrain's gains, and never will be.

PenguinBot's real arm runs kP = 0.2 against a setpoint of 9.0 rotations, and that number was found by running this exact recipe on the actual arm, not by copying it from anywhere. Every lesson from here to Unit 11 uses it.

On step sizes

You may hear an older team member say to raise the gain "in steps of about 5." That advice came from a mechanism whose gains ran on a much larger scale than anything on this page, where a step of 5 would be a factor of two hundred. Take the method, which is to raise slowly and change one thing, and ignore the literal number. Start from whatever is currently in the file and double it.

Neither neighbouring run overshot. Overshoot needs enough gain to carry the mechanism past the target while it is still moving, and both of these are below that.

9 Common mistakes

Watch out for these
  • Judging a tune by settling time alone. Run 5 settled fastest and was the worst run on the page. Check overshoot and direction changes as well.
  • Assuming higher gain is better. It is, up to a point. Past that point you get overshoot, then ringing, then a mechanism vibrating against its own setpoint.
  • Reaching for I and D when P disappoints. Add a term for a specific symptom. Adding both at once means you cannot tell what did what, which is the whole reason for the recipe.
  • Trusting the clamp to keep you safe. It limits force. Run 5 stayed under 0.8 the entire time.
  • Changing two gains to save time. You get a result you cannot attribute and have to test both again. Slower overall, not faster.
  • Assuming a tune is permanent. Re-check after a mechanical change, a new gearbox, or added weight. The gains describe the mechanism, and the mechanism changed.
  • Tuning in the sim and deploying the numbers. The sim has no gravity, no game piece and no gearbox slop. It teaches the method only.

Tune the arm and keep a log

Your Arm class from last lesson has a hand-written P controller with a gain you never justified. Justify it.

  1. Use the harness above to find a gain that gets inside tolerance and stays there, with zero direction changes and no overshoot.
  2. Keep a tuning log. For every gain you try, record the settle tick or "never", the overshoot, the direction changes, and a one sentence verdict. Minimum four rows.
  3. Set the gain in your Arm class.
  4. Write the sentence you would say to a teammate, covering what the value is and what happens if they raise it.
The log is the deliverable, not the number

The gain is one line. The record of how you found it is what lets the next person change it safely, and it is what you will be asked for when the arm misbehaves at a competition. Most rows in a real tuning log are failures. That ratio is normal.

// Tuned on the real arm with the recipe in Unit 6.
// Reaches 9.0 rotations cleanly, no overshoot, no direction reversals.

What you would tell a teammate:

"The arm runs a gain of 0.2, which reaches the scoring setpoint cleanly with no overshoot. If you raise it, watch the direction reversals rather than the settling time, because a gain that is far too high looks fast and is actually vibrating the mechanism. If you change the arm's weight or its gearing, re-tune it, and change one number at a time."

Note that the comment records the boundary, not just the value. Six months from now the useful information is not that the gain is 0.2. It is that 0.5 is where it starts going wrong.
