1 Why this matters

PathPlanner is approximate, and it has to be

Our curriculum's description of the tool ends with one word carrying all the weight: it computes the motor power and swerve commands to follow your route, approximately.

Think about what it is working from. Your typed-in weight, moment of inertia and dimensions, which are close rather than exact. It cannot know that the carpet is more worn in front of the scoring station, that your battery is at 12.1 volts instead of 12.8, that a wheel tread has glazed over, or that somebody bumped you in the first two seconds.

So a path gets you close. The prescription that follows immediately in the source is to pair it with alignment and position correction code, which is your PID and your vision. Which you built last unit.

That is the shape of a real autonomous routine. Drive approximately, then correct precisely. A team that trusts the path alone scores in practice and misses at competition.

2 Why autonomous is last

Autonomous is the opening seconds of a match, where the robot runs itself with nobody driving. Points are on the line and nobody can help it.

You cannot build a reliable autonomous until mechanics and vision work

This is the fourth time this course has said it, and it is the point our curriculum repeats more than any other.

The reason is mechanical rather than motivational. An autonomous routine is literally a sequence of your mechanism and vision commands. Until those commands work, there is nothing to sequence. A team that starts building auto in week two ends up debugging the arm through an autonomous routine, which is the hardest possible way to debug an arm.

Verify for current season

How long the autonomous period lasts and what it scores are game-specific and change every year. This lesson deliberately states no duration and no point values. Read this season's game manual.

3 PathPlanner is an app

It is a desktop application, not a library you write against. It shows you a visual field. You place a start pose and draw the robot's route through waypoints. It saves path files that your robot code later loads by name.

Like the swerve generator, it needs the robot's physical facts before it can compute anything: weight, moment of inertia and dimensions. It is simulating your robot to work out what your robot can actually do.

Wrong numbers there produce a path the robot cannot follow, and there is no error message. Just a robot that overshoots every corner.

Why there is no PathPlanner code on this page

Our sources name the concepts, which are paths, named commands, wait commands and the autonomous picker, and give no PathPlanner API at all. Registering named commands and assembling a routine both need real method signatures, and PathPlanner's have changed between versions.

Printing a guess would teach you a call that does not exist. The workflow below and the approximate-then-correct pattern are the stable parts, and they are what this lesson is actually for. Get the API from the current PathPlanner documentation.

4 The start pose is a promise

A path begins at a stated position and heading. If the robot is placed on the field somewhere else, every subsequent movement is offset by that error, and the error compounds along the route.

what you drew what happened [start] [start] | \ robot placed 30 cm left | \ v v [in front of goal] [30 cm left of the goal] and every later waypoint inherits the same offset
Three units, one idea

You have now met this bug three times.

  • Unit 5. A relative encoder that was never homed. Position 9.0 means nothing.
  • Unit 9. A gyro zeroed while the robot faced sideways. Every stick input is rotated.
  • Unit 11. A robot placed somewhere other than the declared start pose. Every waypoint is offset.

A position means nothing until you know where zero is. That is the strongest recurring idea in this whole track, and all three cases are solved the same way: establish zero deliberately, before it matters, and check it.

The practical consequence is a game-day item rather than a code change. The robot has to be placed on the field the same way every single match. Teams mark the floor, line up against the field's own markings, and have somebody whose pre-match job is to check it. It is a programming problem solved with tape.

Named commands are the commands you already wrote

This should land as a relief. ScoreSequence, AimAtTagCommand, ArmToScoreCommand. You register them under names and the routine calls them.

Autonomous adds almost no new code. That is what "auto is a sequence of the commands you already wrote" means in practice, and it is the whole payoff of having built things in this order.

6 The workflow, end to end

1. Enter the robot's physical constants into PathPlanner. 2. Place the start pose, matching where the robot will really be placed. 3. Draw the path or paths. 4. In your code, REGISTER your existing commands as named commands. 5. Assemble paths, named commands and waits into a routine. 6. Expose the routine through the autonomous picker in RobotContainer. 7. Test it. On the actual floor. Repeatedly. 8. Add vision correction where precision matters.
Step 7 is a step, not a formality

An autonomous routine that has been run twice is not tested.

Battery voltage changes how hard the robot accelerates. Carpet condition changes how it grips. Starting position varies by a few centimetres every time a human puts it down. A routine that works once can fail in a way that scores zero, and you will not know which until you have run it enough times to see the variation.

7 Drive approximately, correct precisely

Path: drive to roughly in front of the goal (approximate, fast, covers metres) | v AimAtTagCommand: close the last few degrees (precise, slow, covers degrees) | v ScoreSequence: the commands you already had

The path covers three metres in two seconds and does not need precision to do it. Vision covers the last few degrees, where precision matters most.

Doing it the other way round fails in both directions. Using vision for the long haul would be slow and would require a tag in view the whole way. Using the path for the final alignment would miss.

The split goes exactly where the cost of an error starts to exceed the cost of the time it takes to correct it.

9 Common mistakes

Watch out for these
  • Expecting the robot to go exactly where you drew. It gets close, from approximate physical constants, and it cannot see the carpet or the battery. Close, then correct.
  • Writing new commands for autonomous. It reuses the commands you already wrote for teleop, which is the whole reason it is built last.
  • Believing the robot knows where it is. It knows where it thinks it is, starting from the pose you declared. Vision is how it checks.
  • Building autonomous while the arm is still being tuned. Auto is a sequence of those commands. Until they work there is nothing to sequence.
  • Thinking one successful run means it works. Run it ten times, on a fresh battery and a flat one, from a slightly wrong starting position.
  • Using waits to sequence steps. Use them for physical settling time. For "has the previous step finished," that is what isFinished() is for.
Build it · PenguinBot

Design PenguinBot's autonomous

PenguinBot can drive field-centrically, aim at a tag, and score from one button. Every command an autonomous routine needs already exists. Nothing sequences them without a human. Write the design document, and the next lesson builds it.

  1. Write the routine as an ordered list of paths, named commands and waits, with a one-line justification for each.
  2. State the start pose in words, where on the field and facing which way, and how the team will guarantee the robot is placed there.
  3. List which existing commands need registering. Note how many new command classes you have to write. The answer should be small, and if it is not, something earlier went wrong.
  4. Mark which steps are approximate and which are precise, and say why the split is where it is.
  5. Write the test plan. What you would vary between runs, and how many runs before you trust it.
Reveal the solution

1. The routine

#BlockWhy
1Path, start line to in front of the scoring positionFast, covers most of the distance. Approximate is fine here
2Named command, aimAtTagCloses the last few degrees precisely, which the path cannot
3Named command, scoreGamePieceArm up, outtake, wait, arm down. Already built and tested in teleop
4Wait, a fraction of a secondLet the robot settle before moving off so the piece is not dragged
5Path, scoring position to out of the wayClears the space and positions us for teleop

2. Start pose. On the start line at the left-hand field marking, facing the far end of the field. Guaranteed by floor tape lined up with the field's own markings, a photo in the pit as a reference, and one person whose pre-match job is to check the placement and say it out loud.

This is the same "what does zero mean" problem as homing an encoder and zeroing the gyro, and like both of those it is solved before the match rather than during it.

3. Named commands to register: scoreGamePiece mapped to ScoreSequence, and aimAtTag mapped to AimAtTagCommand.

New command classes to write: zero. Everything is already built and already tested in teleop, which is exactly what building auto last buys you.

4. Approximate versus precise.

  • Steps 1 and 5, the paths, are approximate. They cover metres, and being 10 cm off does not matter because step 2 will fix it.
  • Step 2, vision, is precise. It covers a few degrees, and there 10 cm does matter.

The split sits at the point where the cost of an error starts to exceed the cost of the time spent correcting it.

5. Test plan. Ten runs minimum, varying deliberately.

  • Three on a fresh battery and three on a half-drained one, because voltage changes acceleration.
  • Two from a deliberately mis-placed start, five to ten centimetres off. Does vision recover it?
  • Two with the tag partially blocked. Does the validity guard do its job, or does the routine hang?

Trust it when it scores in at least nine of them, and when the one failure is understood rather than shrugged at.
